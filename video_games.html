Video Games: Games but with Video

Humanity has been playing games since likely the very inception of the species, with some of the oldest games found dating back millenia (the oldest found board game, the Royal Game of Ur, was being played in Mesopotamia over four thousand years ago)! Play is a fundamental developmental activity in not only humans but many other animals, and functions both as a tool for learning and as a powerful social activity.

It is natural, then, that one of the first things we developed at the advent of modern computing was the video game.

What the Hell is Even That?

A video game is a type of game (or so I am told) played on a computer, using some form of input device (such as a keyboard or controller) to allow users ('players' or 'g*mers') to interact with the game. Video games provide audiovisual feedback to users, often on a video screen (hence why they call them 'computer games'). They can be played on a wide variety of computers; from personal computers ('PCs' to the youth), to dedicated consoles, to even mobile phones if you're a freak.

Video Games: Origins

The earliest invention that could conceivably be called a video game was Thomas T. Goldsmith Jr.'s 'Cathode-ray tube amusement device', patented in 1947. This was the earliest conceived interactive electronic game, and gameplay would have involved using knobs to adjust the trajectory of a shell such that it hit plastic targets placed on the screen. Alas, this game was never actually manufactured outside of a few handmade prototypes.

Similarly, the earliest known written video game was Turochamp, a chess program written by Alan Turing and David Champernowne in 1948. While the code was unfortunately never run on an actual computer, Turing hand traced it in a game against Alick Glennie in 1952.

Throughout the 1950s, computers where generally only available to research institutions such as universities, and so near all programs written for them were for research or demonstration purposes. One of the earliest games written for one of these computers, and the first to be demonstrated to the public, was 'Bertie the Brain', a noughts and crosses program written in 1950 intended to demonstrate the newly developed Additron tube.

The first game to be readily accessible outside of a single research institution, and arguably the game responsible for popularising video game development as a whole, was 'Spacewar!', developed by Harvard and MIT employees Martin Graetz, Steve Russell, and Wayne Wiitanen in 1962 for the PDP-1. This game was developed to meet three requirements:

  1. Use as many of the PDP-1's resources as possible
  2. Be consistantly interesting
  3. Be entertaining

'Spacewar!' was a two player game that simulated a fight between two spaceships, with a randomly generated starry background. It was ported to several minicomputers across American research institutions, and inspired many programmers to create their own games (something Russell has gone on record to say he is most proud of about the game).
